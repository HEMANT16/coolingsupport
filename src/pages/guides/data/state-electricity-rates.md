When calculating the return on investment (ROI) of a high-efficiency 18 SEER2 heat pump or estimating the hourly running cost of a central air conditioner, using national averages will lead to incorrect conclusions. Electricity prices are highly regional, governed by local fuel mixes, state-level regulations, and grid infrastructure.

This database provides the average residential electricity rates (cents per kilowatt-hour) for all 50 US states and the District of Columbia. The data is compiled directly from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) *Electric Power Monthly* reports, updated for **2026**.

---

## How Utility Tariffs are Structured

When a homeowner reads their electric bill, the "supply rate" is only part of the equation. To determine your true cost of cooling, you must use the **Effective Rate**:

$$\text{Effective Rate (\$/kWh)} = \frac{\text{Total Utility Bill (\$) - Fixed Connection Fees (\)}}{\text{Total Consumption (kWh)}}$$

A standard residential tariff is broken down into:
1.  **Generation/Supply Charge**: The cost to generate the electricity (per kWh).
2.  **Transmission & Distribution (T&D) Charge**: The cost to move electricity over high-voltage lines and deliver it to your home meter (per kWh).
3.  **Customer Charge**: A flat monthly connection fee (typically $10–$25) regardless of usage.
4.  **Taxes & Environmental Surcharges**: State and local utility taxes (often structured as a percentage of usage or flat per-kWh rate).

The rates listed below reflect the **all-in average retail rate per kWh**, which includes T&D fees and taxes, to ensure your financial calculations are as accurate as possible.

## National Extremes (Top 5 States)

### Most Expensive States (High-Cooling Cost Risk)
1.  **Hawaii (42.2¢/kWh)**: Remote island grid reliant on imported diesel fuel generation.
2.  **California (33.2¢/kWh)**: High transmission costs, wildfire mitigation surcharges, and aggressive carbon-free mandates.
3.  **Connecticut (30.5¢/kWh)**: High natural gas delivery prices combined with regional grid congestion.
4.  **Massachusetts (29.5¢/kWh)**: Relies on gas import infrastructure, especially during winter heating spikes.
5.  **Rhode Island (28.2¢/kWh)**: Similar grid constraints to the rest of the ISO-New England network.

### Least Expensive States (Low-Cooling Cost Risk)
1.  **Idaho (11.2¢/kWh)**: Abundant and cheap municipal hydroelectric generation from the Columbia River basin.
2.  **North Dakota (11.8¢/kWh)**: Localized lignite coal, natural gas, and heavy wind generation capacity.
3.  **Utah (11.8¢/kWh)**: Low cost of coal and gas generation combined with extensive solar expansion.
4.  **Washington (11.9¢/kWh)**: The nation's largest producer of hydroelectricity (Grand Coulee Dam).
5.  **Wyoming (12.5¢/kWh)**: Large coal and natural gas reserves resulting in low local generation costs.
